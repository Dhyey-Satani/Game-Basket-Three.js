Mercury on Esports Training: Beyond the Tactics Sheet

In the high-stakes world of competitive gaming, the difference between a win and a loss often comes down to a split-second decision. Professional player Mercury is challenging the traditional coaching methods that rely too heavily on rigid instruction.

Category: Esports News Published: October 2, 2026 Updated: October 2, 2026 4 min read

The limitation of rigid tactics

In many professional esports organizations, practice sessions are often dominated by the study of tactical sheets. These documents outline every movement, every utility usage, and every positioning requirement for a given map. While structure is essential for building a foundation, Mercury argues that over-reliance on these sheets can actually stifle a team's growth and adaptability during live matches.

When players are trained to follow a script, they become highly efficient in controlled environments. However, the reality of professional competition is chaotic. Opponents do not always play the way the tactics sheet predicts, and unexpected variables can render a pre-planned strategy useless in seconds. If a player has only been trained to follow instructions, they may freeze when the situation deviates from the plan.

Prioritizing player judgment calls

Mercury believes the true purpose of practice should be to empower players to make their own judgment calls. Instead of simply memorizing where to stand, players should understand the why behind every movement. This deeper level of comprehension allows them to react intuitively to the flow of the game, making decisions that are based on real-time information rather than outdated instructions.

The purpose of practice should be so they can make their own judgment calls, not just be limited to what's written on the tactics sheet.

By shifting the focus from rote memorization to cognitive development, coaches can cultivate players who are much harder to prepare for. A team that can think on its feet is far more dangerous than a team that simply executes a set of pre-determined plays. This mental flexibility is what separates the good teams from the world-class champions.

Developing intuition through practice

Building intuition is not something that happens overnight; it requires a specific type of training environment. Mercury suggests that practice sessions should include scenarios that force players to solve problems under pressure. Rather than telling a player what to do when an opponent pushes a certain angle, coaches should present the scenario and let the player find the solution themselves.

This approach encourages a growth mindset. When players are allowed to fail during practice, they learn the consequences of their decisions in a safe environment. This iterative process of trial and error is the most effective way to build the muscle memory of the mind, allowing instinct to take over when the pressure of a tournament is at its peak.

The role of the modern coach

This philosophy also requires a shift in how we view the role of the coach. In the past, a coach might have been seen as a commander who dictates every move. In the modern era, Mercury's perspective suggests that the coach should act more like a facilitator or a mentor. Their job is to provide the tools and the framework, but ultimately to step back and let the players lead.

A coach who focuses on judgment calls helps build a more resilient team culture. When players feel ownership over their decisions, they are more engaged and more confident. This sense of agency is crucial for maintaining morale during long tournament runs and difficult losing streaks. A team that trusts its own instincts is a team that can weather any storm.

Connecting decision making to performance

It is important to recognize that decision-making and mechanical skill are two sides of the same coin. You can have the fastest aim in the world, but if you are in the wrong position because you followed a tactic sheet blindly, your mechanical skill will never matter. High-level play requires a perfect synergy between what your hands do and what your brain decides.
